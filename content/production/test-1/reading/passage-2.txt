Sleep and Memory Consolidation

Researchers have long observed a link between sleep and the ability to retain newly learned information. Recent laboratory studies have begun to clarify exactly how this process, known as memory consolidation, works. In one widely cited study, participants who slept recalled 20 percent more word pairs than those who stayed awake. The stage of sleep most associated with memory consolidation is called slow-wave sleep. Researchers used EEG recordings to observe brain activity during sleep. The study involved 48 adult participants in total. A short nap of only 90 minutes produced a measurable improvement in recall. The researchers propose that memories are transferred from the hippocampus to the cortex during sleep. Sleep deprivation for a single night reduced recall accuracy by roughly 30 percent.
