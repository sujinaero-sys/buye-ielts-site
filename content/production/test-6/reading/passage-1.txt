The Growth of Vertical Farming

Vertical farming involves cultivating plants in stacked layers within controlled indoor environments. Proponents argue it offers a sustainable solution to food security in growing urban populations. Vertical farming facilities often operate inside repurposed urban warehouses. Controlled indoor agriculture uses up to 95 percent less water than traditional farming. Artificial LED lighting allows crops to grow for 24 hours a day. Hydroponic systems deliver nutrients directly to plant roots. Leafy greens and herbs currently account for the majority of commercial crops. The initial capital cost of equipping a vertical farm remains high due to expensive technology. A major environmental benefit cited by researchers is eliminating agricultural runoff, which severely pollutes freshwater ecosystems in open-field farming. Critics emphasize that high energy demands are primarily driven by continuous climate control and artificial lighting systems inside the facility. The passage discusses global developments in indoor farming without specifying where the first commercial facility opened. Because indoor environments are completely sealed against pests, vertical farms do not require chemical pesticide applications. Grain crops such as wheat and rice are not grown profitably indoors because their low market value cannot offset high electricity costs. Automated robotic sensors are increasingly deployed to monitor plant health and growth rates across vertical stacks. Advancements in energy-efficient LED lights have actually reduced electricity costs for vertical farm operators over the past decade.
